# Chapter 20: Romans 9 — "not all who are from Israel are Israel"

Part III ended by putting the original question back on the table: Israel, the nations, and what the commission actually required of whom.

This Part answers it by working through the three chapters where Paul takes it up directly. And I want to say at the outset that I came to Romans 9 already knowing what it meant, which by now should be a warning sign to anyone reading this book carefully.

## Paul begins with anguish, not with a verdict

The first thing Romans 9 does is not doctrinal. Paul says he has great sorrow and unceasing anguish in his heart, and that he could wish himself accursed and cut off from Messiah for the sake of his kinsmen according to the flesh.

That is not the opening of a man announcing that a people has been set aside. Whatever Paul is about to argue, he is arguing it in grief, about his own family, and the grief is the reason the argument exists.

## And he calls them Israelites, in the present tense

> They **are** Israelites, and to them belong the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the giving of the law, the worship, and the promises (Romans 9:4)

The Greek is <span class="gr">Ἰσραηλῖται</span> *(Israēlitai)*. Not *they used to be*. Not *until Messiah came*.

Paul is writing after the death, the resurrection, the ascension, Pentecost and the inclusion of Gentiles. And he calls his **unbelieving** ethnic kinsmen Israelites, and then piles honour on honour rather than speaking of their covenantal history with contempt — the patriarchs are theirs, and Messiah Himself comes from them according to the flesh.

::: finding
**Finding — a brake on 9:6, established from 9:4, two verses earlier**

Whatever covenantal change Messiah brought, Paul has **not** reassigned the word so that unbelieving Jews can no longer hold it.

Any reading of verse 6 that requires *Israel* to have been transferred away from them has to get past verse 4 first, and verse 4 is in the same breath. I had never noticed that, because I had always arrived at Romans 9 already looking for verse 6.
:::

And a detail in the inventory that arrived as independent confirmation of something this book reached seven stages earlier from a different direction: Paul writes **the covenants**, plural — <span class="gr">διαθῆκαι</span> *(diathēkai)*. Abrahamic, Sinaitic, Davidic, and the new one promised through Jeremiah. That is the one-covenantal-story-containing-multiple-covenants refinement from Chapter 13, in Paul's own grammar, and I did not go looking for it here.

## Only now does the problem appear

With that inventory in place, Paul states the difficulty, and it is worth stating precisely because almost every argument I have had about this chapter mislocates it.

Paul's immediate problem is **not** *who is the Church?* It is: **how can Yahweh's promises to Israel remain trustworthy when so many Israelites stand outside their messianic fulfilment?**

That is a question about the reliability of God, raised by a Jew, about Jews. Reading it as a question about ecclesiology is answering something Paul did not ask.

## The wordplay, which English mostly hides

> **οὐ γὰρ πάντες οἱ ἐξ Ἰσραὴλ οὗτοι Ἰσραήλ**
>
> Very literally: *for not all those from Israel — these are Israel* (Romans 9:6)

Paul uses *Israel* twice, deliberately. There is Israel₁, those who are **from** Israel, and Israel₂, those who constitute Israel in the sense relevant to the promise.

So the question is what distinguishes the second from the first. And this is exactly where I jumped too quickly for years.

## Paul answers with Isaac, not with Gentile Christians

> and not all are children of Abraham because they are his offspring, but "Through Isaac shall your offspring be named" (Romans 9:7)

Who is being contrasted? **Ishmael and Isaac.** Both biologically Abraham's.

Verse 8's *children of the promise* sounds like something that could include Gentiles, and elsewhere Paul does connect Gentiles to the Abrahamic promise. But here his first example is not Jew against Gentile. It is one son of Abraham against another.

::: evidence
**How strongly evidence bears**

Paul's first demonstration **ESTABLISHES** that biological descent from Abraham has never, by itself, determined the line through which the covenant promise proceeds.

It **DOES NOT ESTABLISH** that *Israel now means the Church*. That is a much larger claim, and Paul has not made it here. The distinction he draws in 9:7 runs *inside* Abraham's household, and importing a Jew/Gentile frame onto it is reading the conclusion back into the example.
:::

That is considerably more precise than what I had been saying, and considerably less useful to the position I held when I arrived.

## Malachi, and why three kinds of evidence cannot be one kind

Then Paul quotes Jacob loved, Esau hated — and this is where the chapter taught me something about method rather than about Israel.

::: commentary
**Researcher's commentary — the case the never-collapse rule exists for**

I asked three different questions of the same quotation and got three different kinds of answer.

*What does "Esau" mean?* That is a **lexical** question, and it yields nothing that solves Romans 9.

*What is Paul's immediate context?* That is **contextual**, and it tells me he is discussing Israel and the reliability of Yahweh's promise.

*Where did the quotation come from?* That is **intertextual**, and it is the one that changes things. Malachi 1:2-3 is not speaking about two individuals deciding their eternal destinies. Malachi is speaking about **Edom** — a nation — and its territory. And behind Malachi stands Genesis 25:23, where Rebekah is told that **two nations** are in her womb.

The intertextual evidence materially alters how much I am entitled to infer from Paul's words, and neither the lexical nor the contextual work would ever have produced it.

If I had merged these three into an undifferentiated "evidence," the Edom and nations dimension would simply have been invisible. Not refuted — invisible. That is the specific loss the never-collapse rule exists to prevent, and this is the clearest case of it I have hit.
:::

I want to be careful about what that does and does not buy. It does not establish that Romans 9 is *only* about nations and not about individuals; Paul is plainly applying the pattern to individuals standing outside the messianic fulfilment. It establishes that the quotation arrives carrying a corporate and territorial history that a reader working only from Paul's sentence would never suspect.

## Then Paul closes the escape route I wanted

Having found that, my instinct was to soften the passage into a statement purely about nations and roles, which would have made it much more comfortable.

Paul does not permit it. He goes on to Exodus 33:19 — *I will have mercy on whom I have mercy* — and then to Pharaoh, and then to the potter and the clay.

::: historical
**Historical context**

Exodus 33:19 is not spoken in a vacuum. **The golden calf has just happened.** Israel has broken the covenant almost immediately after receiving it, Moses is interceding, and the mercy formula is Yahweh's answer in that situation.

Which means the verse Paul reaches for to make his point about sovereign mercy is a verse in which **Israel itself survives only by mercy**. That is not a text about God choosing some people and rejecting others from outside. It is a text about a covenant people that had already forfeited its standing and was spared.
:::

And the potter and the clay is not Paul's image either. It is Jeremiah's and Isaiah's, and in Jeremiah 18 the imagery is explicitly about Yahweh's authority over **the house of Israel**, with the outcome conditioned on that nation's response.

::: evidence
**How strongly evidence bears — the brake, in both directions**

What **CAN** be established: Paul uses prophetic imagery whose scriptural background concerns Yahweh's sovereign authority over covenant peoples and nations, Israel in particular.

What **CANNOT** be established from that background alone: that the passage therefore carries no individual application. Showing that a determinist reading overreaches does not establish the corporate-only reading in its place — that would be the Anti-Replacement Principle again, and I have now caught myself at it enough times to expect it.
:::

## One verification that changed less than I hoped

I did the grammatical work on Romans 9:22 and 23, because a great deal of argument rests on whether the two are parallel.

They are not parallel. Verse 23 has an active verb with God as its subject. Verse 22 has a participle that is morphologically ambiguous between middle and passive, and it names no agent.

That asymmetry is real and established. What it **means** is not. A middle voice would suggest the vessels fitted themselves; a passive would suggest they were fitted; and Greek perfect middle and passive forms are frequently identical, so the ambiguity may be a morphological accident rather than an authorial choice.

I am recording it at exactly that strength. It is a fact about the grammar that constrains confident readings on both sides, and it is not a conclusion.

## And then the chapter refuses to stay inside ethnic Israel

Having spent the chapter insisting that Paul is talking about Israel, I have to report where Paul goes next, because it cuts the other way.

He quotes Hosea — *those who were not my people I will call my people* — and applies it to the calling of Gentiles. Then he quotes Isaiah on a remnant of Israel being saved.

So both are in the chapter. The vocabulary of Israel's restoration is applied to Gentiles, and the hope held out for ethnic Israel is a remnant rather than a replacement. Neither half is an intrusion into the other.

::: openquestion
**Open question — held open, and it will cost something later**

Paul quotes Hosea in a form closer to the Septuagint than to the Hebrew text, and this is not the only place in the New Testament where that happens.

For a method that says *go to the source languages*, that is a real complication rather than a curiosity: the apostles are quoting a **translation**, and building arguments on it. I am flagging it here, not resolving it, and it returns two chapters from now, where Paul turns out to match neither the Hebrew nor the Greek, and where James's use of Amos 9 at the Jerusalem Council has to be faced.
:::

::: whereweare
**Where we are.** Paul opens in anguish over his own kinsmen and calls them Israelites in the present tense, after Pentecost, which brakes any reading of 9:6 that transfers the word away from them. His first demonstration is Ishmael against Isaac, not Jew against Gentile, and it establishes that biological descent never by itself determined the promised line. The Malachi quotation carries an Edom-and-nations background that only intertextual work makes visible. Exodus 33 turns out to be a golden-calf text in which Israel itself survives by mercy. And the chapter ends with Hosea applied to Gentiles and a remnant held out for Israel, with neither half swallowing the other.
:::

::: earned
**What it earned.** I expected Romans 9 to settle this and it did not; it sharpened the question instead. Which means the next chapter cannot be avoided any longer. Romans 10 is the strongest text against the position I brought into this investigation, and I am going to run straight at it.
:::
