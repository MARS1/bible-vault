# Chapter 21: Romans 10 — the strongest text against where I started

I said at the beginning of this investigation that I would run at the texts most likely to destroy my position rather than around them. This is where that promise gets tested, and I want the framing on the record before the evidence, so nobody has to take my word afterward that I went in unprotected.

Here is my original position, stated as I actually held it. When my relative pressed me with Mark 16 and Matthew 28 and James 4:17 and Acts 8:4, I answered that preaching was not my calling — that gifts are distributed unevenly, that Ephesians 4:11 names evangelist as a specific function rather than a universal one, and that 1 Peter 3:15 asks readiness to answer rather than initiative to proclaim.

And Romans 10 contains this:

> How then will they call on him in whom they have not believed? And how are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone preaching? (Romans 10:14)

That is the passage a person quotes to someone like me. I knew it was coming. This chapter is where I stopped walking around it.

## The subject has not changed

The first thing that matters is structural, and it is easy to lose because chapter divisions are later conveniences.

Romans 10 opens with Paul saying his heart's desire and prayer to God for **them** is that they may be saved. Them is Israel. The subject has not changed from chapter 9, and it does not change before chapter 11 picks it up again.

So Romans 10 is not a disconnected missionary handbook that happens to sit between two chapters about Israel. Its immediate subject is Israel's response to Messiah, and that governs how the preaching chain in verses 14 and 15 may be read.

That observation does not settle anything by itself. It sets the frame, and the frame is Paul's, not mine.

## Their problem is not a lack of zeal

Paul says they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge. Whatever is wrong is not indifference. That matters because it removes the easiest caricature available in this argument, and I had used that caricature.

## Deuteronomy 30 is doing the work, again

Then Paul's argument about the righteousness of faith turns out to be built out of Torah rather than against it. He reaches for Deuteronomy 30 — the word is very near you, in your mouth and in your heart — and uses it to describe the nearness of the message of faith.

This is the third or fourth time in this book that Paul's critique of the misuse of Torah has turned out to be made from **inside** Torah. I am noting the pattern because it is consistent enough to be a feature of how Paul argues, and because it cuts against a reading of Paul I brought with me.

## Romans 10:4, which cannot be settled in English

> For Christ is the **end** of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes (Romans 10:4, ESV)

::: wordstudy
**Original language**

<span class="gr">τέλος</span> *(telos)* — and the English word chosen to render it carries the argument.

*Telos* can mean **termination**, the point at which something stops. It can also mean **goal**, **completion**, the end *toward which* something was aimed — the sense in which a road has an end, or a purpose is achieved.

The ESV's *end* preserves the ambiguity of the Greek only by accident, because the English word has both senses too. But readers do not hear both. They hear the first.

**Romans 10:4 cannot be settled from the English word "end."** Anyone quoting *end of the law* as though the verse said *Torah is abolished* has substituted one sense of an ambiguous noun for the verse itself. That is not an argument about Paul. It is an argument about a translator's word.
:::

I am not resolving *telos* here. Chapter 15's definition of fulfilment — a pattern reaching its reality — is obviously relevant, and I am deliberately not cashing it, because using my own earlier conclusion to disambiguate a contested word would be circular.

## Then access universalizes

Paul goes on to say there is no distinction between Jew and Greek, that the same Lord is Lord of all and bestows riches on all who call on Him.

Expansion without erasure — the same pattern Part III kept producing. Israel remains the immediate problem of the chapter; salvation in Messiah is not ethnically restricted. Both at once, again, with neither half permitted to consume the other.

## The chain, which I am not going to soften

> How then will they call on him in whom they have not believed? And how are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone preaching? And how are they to preach unless they are sent? (Romans 10:14-15)

Formally: **call ← believe ← hear ← proclaimer ← sending.**

This is a genuine and strong establishment of the necessity of proclamation. I am not going to soften it, qualify it into uselessness, or discover that it means something other than what it says. Faith ordinarily comes through someone announcing something. That is Paul's claim and it is plain.

If the chapter ended at *someone preaching*, my original position would be in serious trouble.

## But Paul adds one more link, and it is the pivot

It does not end there. Paul adds a fourth link that I had never once heard quoted in any of these conversations, including by me: **and how are they to preach unless they are sent?**

::: evidence
**How strongly evidence bears**

| Proposition | Status |
|---|---|
| Faith ordinarily requires proclamation | **ESTABLISHED** |
| Proclaimers are **sent** | **ESTABLISHED** — Paul himself adds it as the final link |
| Every believer personally occupies the sent-herald role | **NOT ESTABLISHED by this text** |
| Every believer is commanded here to proclaim to every region | **NOT ESTABLISHED by this text** |

The final link **cuts against** the universal-obligation reading rather than for it. Paul does not stop at *someone must preach*. He adds *unless they are sent*, which introduces commission — and commission is a **restriction** on the set of heralds, not an expansion of it.
:::

That is the pivot of this whole chapter, and I want to be honest about how it felt. The text most likely to overturn my position contains, in its own last clause, the distinction my position was trying to draw. I did not put it there and I had not known it was there.

::: wordstudy
**Original language**

<span class="gr">κηρύσσω</span> *(kēryssō)* — to herald, to proclaim publicly. It carries public-announcement force. It is not "mention in conversation."

<span class="gr">ἀποστέλλω</span> *(apostellō)* — to send. The root of <span class="gr">ἀπόστολος</span> *(apostolos)*, "sent one."

**And the brake, because the word-family link is real and does not prove what I would like it to.** *Apostellō* here does **not** restrict the sending to the Twelve. The vocabulary establishes that commission is in view; it does not establish a closed office.

Both overreadings fail: *the logic involves commission* may not be collapsed into *only apostles may proclaim*, any more than *someone must preach* may be collapsed into *therefore everyone must*.
:::

And Isaiah 52:7, which Paul quotes next about the feet of those who bring good news, is not a generic image either. In Isaiah it is a **covenantal herald** announcing to Zion that her God reigns — a specific figure with a specific message to a specific city.

## And now the part that costs me

Here is where the chapter takes back something larger than what it gave.

Romans 10:18 says: *Their voice has gone out to all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world.* And that verse has been doing quiet work in my position for a long time, because the second phrase is <span class="gr">οἰκουμένη</span> *(oikoumenē)* — the same word as Matthew 24:14.

The bridge writes itself. Paul says the message reached the *oikoumenē*; Matthew 24:14 says the end comes after the good news is proclaimed in the *oikoumenē*; therefore the condition was met in the first century.

I wanted that bridge. So I checked whether Paul chose the word.

**He did not.** Romans 10:18 is a quotation of Psalm 19:4, and the Septuagint of that Psalm reads:

> <span class="gr">εἰς πᾶσαν τὴν **γῆν** ἐξῆλθεν ὁ φθόγγος αὐτῶν καὶ εἰς τὰ πέρατα τῆς **οἰκουμένης** τὰ ῥήματα αὐτῶν</span>

Both <span class="gr">γῆν</span> and <span class="gr">οἰκουμένης</span> are inherited from the Psalm, verbatim. Paul is quoting, not describing.

::: finding
**Finding — against my own hypothesis, and it is the reason this chapter is in the book**

An argument of the form *"Paul says the good news reached the* oikoumenē*, the same word as Matthew 24:14, therefore Matthew 24:14's condition was met"* rests on **a word Paul borrowed from a Psalm about the heavens declaring the glory of God.**

The lexical overlap is real. It is **not independent attestation** of Paul's own geographic assessment, because Paul did not select the term.

This is the never-collapse-evidence-types rule operating on a case where I would have strongly preferred the other outcome. The overlap is **intertextual**, not independent-lexical, and it may not be reported as the latter.
:::

I have spent chapters warning about arguments that borrow strength from a shared word. This was mine, and it does not survive its own test.

## And a translation problem that inverts the whole thing

One more finding, which is the strongest practical argument this project has produced for working in the source languages.

::: textualnote
**Textual note — Romans 10:18, where two good translations map the same words oppositely**

| Greek position | Greek word | ESV renders | CJB renders |
|---|---|---|---|
| first | <span class="gr">γῆν</span> *(gē)* | "earth" ✓ | "**world**" ✗ |
| second | <span class="gr">οἰκουμένης</span> *(oikoumenē)* | "world" ✓ | "**earth**" ✗ |

The CJB reverses the two English terms relative to the Greek word order. Neither translation is *wrong as English*.

**But the practical consequence is severe.** Anyone building the Matthew 24:14 bridge while reading the CJB in English will attach *oikoumenē* to the **wrong half of the verse** — matching Matthew's term to the clause that is actually rendering *gē*.

No amount of care at the English level detects this. Only the Greek does. I had been reading these verses in English for years and would never have found it.
:::

## Role and act, which is where the chapter leaves me

So what do I actually have, after running at the text I was most afraid of?

The chain stands, unsoftened: faith ordinarily comes through proclamation, and proclaimers are sent. The differentiated-calling reading survives — it survives on Paul's own final clause, not on my preference. And the *oikoumenē* bridge that I had been leaning on is gone, killed by the same discipline that saved the other half.

::: finding
**Finding — the formulation that holds both halves**

**Not everyone is an evangelist — and that does not relieve believers of responsibility to speak truthfully about Messiah when faithfulness, opportunity, or direct questioning calls for it.**

The body of Messiah contains differentiated callings, and Scripture does not portray every member as occupying every office. That is real. It is also not a permission slip for silence, and my original position was closer to a permission slip than I would have admitted at the time.
:::

That formulation is not a compromise between the two sides of the original argument. It is what is left when both sides are made to say only what their texts support — and it concedes more to my relative's concern than my opening answer did.

::: whereweare
**Where we are.** Romans 10's subject is still Israel, and its argument about faith-righteousness is built from Deuteronomy rather than against it. *Telos* at 10:4 cannot be settled in English. The preaching chain is genuine and is not softened here — and its final link, *unless they are sent*, introduces commission and restricts the set of heralds rather than expanding it. Against that, 10:18's *oikoumenē* is quoted verbatim from the Septuagint Psalm and is therefore not independent attestation of anything, which removes a bridge I had been using. And the CJB inverts *gē* and *oikoumenē* relative to the Greek, so the bridge cannot even be checked reliably in English.
:::

::: earned
**What it earned.** The text I expected to lose on gave me the distinction I needed and took away an argument I had been relying on. That is what running at something rather than around it actually looks like. Romans 11 is next, and it is where Paul says what he thinks happens to Israel.
:::
