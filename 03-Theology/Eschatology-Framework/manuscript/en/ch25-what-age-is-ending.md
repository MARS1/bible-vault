# Chapter 25: What age is ending?

Part IV ended by clearing the question I had been carrying since the beginning. What it also did was remove my last excuse for postponing this one.

The phrase *the end of the age* had been sitting in front of me since Part II. It is in the disciples' question on the Mount of Olives. It is in the last sentence of Matthew's Gospel. I had read past it for years, because I thought I already knew what it meant, and what I thought it meant was *the end of everything*.

That turns out not to be a translation. It is an assumption I was attaching to an English word.

## The word I had been hearing wrong

::: wordstudy
**Word study — <span class="gr">αἰών</span> and <span class="gr">συντέλεια τοῦ αἰῶνος</span>**

<span class="gr">αἰών</span> *(aiōn)* — an age, an era, an epoch. A period or order of time.

<span class="gr">συντέλεια</span> *(synteleia)* — consummation, completion, culmination. A bringing to an appointed conclusion.

Together: **the consummation of the age.** Not, in the words themselves, the destruction of the earth.

That is not a small clarification. It means the phrase does not answer the question people assume it answers. It raises one instead, and the question is unavoidable once the words are read as they stand: **which age?**
:::

I want to be careful here, because this is the kind of observation that can be used dishonestly. Showing that a phrase *can* mean something narrower is not the same as showing that it *does*. A word study that ends by announcing the result its author wanted is not evidence, it is decoration.

So the question is whether Matthew himself makes the distinction, or whether I am making it for him.

## Yeshua interprets His own parable, so I am not guessing

Matthew 13 contains the one thing I want most in a disputed passage and almost never get: the speaker explaining His own symbolism, on the record, in the same chapter.

> The field is the world [*kosmos*], and the good seed is the sons of the kingdom. The weeds are the sons of the evil one, and the enemy who sowed them is the devil. The harvest is the end of the age [*synteleia tou aiōnos*], and the reapers are angels. (Matthew 13:38–39)

Read that twice, watching the nouns.

::: finding
**Finding — the two words appear in adjacent clauses, and they are not the same word**

The field is the <span class="gr">κόσμος</span>. The harvest is the <span class="gr">συντέλεια τοῦ αἰῶνος</span>.

The field is **not** the age. The harvest is **not** the destruction of the world. Matthew uses both words inside a single interpretation, a few syllables apart, and he uses them for different things.

Every argument this book has made about refusing to collapse *kosmos*, *oikoumenē*, *gē* and *aiōn* into one another now has a warrant from inside the text rather than from my own reasoning about it. And I did not arrive at it by argument. I arrived at it by reading a passage I had read many times, slowly enough to notice which nouns were in it.
:::

The author who uses both words in one sentence is not treating them as interchangeable. Neither may I.

## And the harvest has content I am not allowed to soften

Here is where I have to be honest about which direction the evidence runs, because it does not all run my way.

> Just as the weeds are gathered and burned with fire, so will it be at the end of the age. The Son of Man will send his angels, and they will gather out of his kingdom all causes of sin and all law-breakers, and throw them into the fiery furnace… Then the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father. (Matthew 13:40–43)

::: evidence
**How strongly evidence bears — against the reading I was moving toward**

This puts real pressure on any casual claim that *the end of the age* means simply *the Temple was destroyed, and nothing more.*

There is harvest here. Angelic agency. Separation of the wicked from the righteous. Fiery judgment. The righteous shining.

A first-century reading has to **account for** that material. Waving at it is not accounting for it.

**CONTRADICTS** — the thin version of the hypothesis I was developing, the one where age-ending language cashes out entirely as the fall of Jerusalem with no remainder.

This is recorded at full strength, in the chapter that found it, because a model that only ever collects its own supporting evidence is not being tested.
:::

I am not going to resolve that here. I am going to carry it.

## The dragnet says it a second time

The pattern is not a single occurrence I might be over-reading.

> …a net "gathered fish of every kind… So it will be at the end of the age. The angels will come out and separate the evil from the righteous and throw them into the fiery furnace." (Matthew 13:47–50)

| | Wheat and weeds (13:36–43) | Dragnet (13:47–50) |
|------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| agency | angels | angels |
| action | gathering | gathering |
| result | separation | separation |
| outcome | fiery judgment | fiery judgment |

Matthew's consummation of the age is not merely a way of saying *later*. It carries judicial and separative content, twice, in the same chapter.

## A verse I am deliberately not going to use

Immediately after the dragnet, Matthew writes this:

> "Have you understood all these things?" They said to him, "Yes." And he said to them, "Therefore every scribe who has been trained for the kingdom of heaven is like a master of a house, who brings out of his treasure what is new and what is old." (Matthew 13:51–52)

Not *the old discarded and the new substituted*. A treasure containing both.

That is the most quotable verse in the chapter for the covenant argument I spent Part III building, and it is exactly the kind of verse that gets over-used because it is satisfying. So I am recording it as resonance with the continuity pattern this book has already found, and I am not making it carry anything. If the covenant argument needs this verse, the covenant argument was weaker than I thought.

## The third occurrence, and where Matthew has placed it

Matthew 23 pronounces judgment on *this generation*. Then the Temple: not one stone left upon another. Then, privately, on the Mount of Olives:

> Tell us, when will these things be, and what will be the sign of your coming and of the consummation of the age? (Matthew 24:3)

The same phrase. And by the time Matthew's reader reaches it, chapter 13 has already loaded it with harvest, angels, gathering, separation and judgment.

## The correspondence, and which way the burden runs

| Matthew 13 | Matthew 24 |
|---|---|
| Son of Man | Son of Man |
| <span class="gr">συντέλεια τοῦ αἰῶνος</span> | <span class="gr">συντέλεια τοῦ αἰῶνος</span> |
| angels | angels |
| gathering | gathering |
| separation and judgment | judgment |
| kingdom | kingdom proclamation |

This does not prove every detail of the two passages refers to one event. What it does is shift the burden. Anyone proposing two completely unrelated ends of the age inside a single Gospel has to explain why Matthew keeps surrounding the phrase with the same imagery.

And I want to flag what just happened to my own rule, because it matters more than the result.

The Equivalent-Scope Burden is the principle I adopted earlier: do not treat two different scope words as equivalent without warrant. At Stage 25 it **destroyed** a bridge I was using — Paul never chooses *oikoumenē*, so I could not build on it, and Chapter 21 records what that cost me. Here the same rule runs the other way and supports a connection I want.

A rule that only ever helped me would be a bias with a rule's name on it. The only reason this one is worth anything is that I have already watched it take something away.

## The consequence I did not want

If Matthew 24 belongs substantially to the first-century judgment on Jerusalem, then a question follows that is considerably larger than *Jerusalem was destroyed*:

**Does Matthew 13's harvest belong to that horizon too?**

That asks whether the destruction of the Temple constitutes a covenantal harvest and judgment that Matthew is describing in final-sounding language. It is a legitimate hypothesis. It is not a demonstrated one, and the difference is the whole book.

::: commentary
**A rule I am stating now, before I need it**

If the model calls the fiery furnace symbolic, it does not get to call the angelic gathering literal merely because that reading is convenient.

Whatever principle governs which elements are figurative has to be stated in advance and applied to the elements the model finds inconvenient as well as the ones it finds helpful. I am writing that down here, in the chapter where the temptation first appears, so that a later chapter cannot quietly help itself.
:::

## Daniel 12 becomes unavoidable

I had been hoping to reach Daniel on my own schedule. Matthew 13:43 removed that option.

> the righteous will **shine like the sun** (Matthew 13:43)
>
> those who are wise shall **shine like the brightness of the sky above**; and those who turn many to righteousness, like the stars forever and ever. (Daniel 12:3)

And the material around Daniel 12:3 is not quiet:

> At that time shall arise Michael… there shall be a time of trouble, such as never has been since there was a nation till that time… And many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt. (Daniel 12:1–2)
>
> shut up the words and seal the book, until the time of the end. (Daniel 12:4)
>
> you shall rest and shall stand in your allotted place at the end of the days. (Daniel 12:13)

::: wordstudy
**Word study — <span class="he">וְרַבִּים</span> at Daniel 12:2, recorded and set down**

The Hebrew is <span class="he">וְרַבִּים</span> *(rabbim)* — **many**. Not *all*.

I retrieved this and then deliberately stopped. It is going to matter for how resurrection is counted, and I can already see that it will bear on a line in Revelation 20 about the rest of the dead.

Which is exactly why I am not building on it now. Reaching forward to a passage I have not yet earned the right to read, in order to strengthen a model I am still assembling, is the precise failure this method exists to prevent.

**Recorded. Not used.**
:::

If Matthew's age-ending harvest echoes Daniel 12, then Daniel's resurrection language becomes a question I cannot dodge — and where Daniel locates that resurrection becomes a test this model has to pass rather than a detail it can route around. That may turn out to be the hardest test in the whole investigation.

## The fourth occurrence

> I am with you all the days, until the consummation of the age. (Matthew 28:20)

Matthew has now attached this phrase to harvest, judgment, angels and separation in chapter 13; to the Temple judgment, the coming, the gathering and *this generation* in chapter 24; and now to the **duration of the commission given to the eleven.**

## What I actually have, stated as a model and not as a conclusion

| Stage in Matthew | Content |
|---|---|
| Matthew 10 | mission restricted primarily to Israel |
| Matthew 13 | harvest at the consummation of the age |
| Matthew 23 | judgment pronounced on *this generation* |
| Matthew 24 | Temple destruction, proclamation through the *oikoumenē*, coming and gathering, *this generation* |
| Matthew 28 | the risen Messiah commissions the eleven to the nations, present until the consummation of the age |

Israel-centred mission, then Messiah rejected, crucified and raised, then a mission to the nations, then proclamation through the inhabited world, then a covenantal harvest and judgment, then the consummation of the age.

That is a genuine textual model, and I want to be exact about what makes it one. It is not *preterism says so*. Matthew supplied the pieces and Matthew supplied them in that order. But assembled is not proven, and the next section is why.

## And here is what it has to survive

::: openquestion
**Open question — the objections, named here so they cannot be dropped later**

1. Matthew 13 sounds like ultimate judgment rather than covenantal transition.
2. Daniel 12 sounds like resurrection of the dead.
3. Matthew 24 contains cosmic imagery and a gathering.
4. Matthew speaks elsewhere of judgment in ways that may reach past AD 70.
5. Believers have understood Matthew 28's disciple-making as ongoing for the entire period since. That is a fact about reception rather than about the text, but it is a real fact and it deserves better than dismissal.

A serious first-century reading has to explain these **better** than the alternatives do. If it cannot, the model gets revised rather than defended.

I am stating them in the chapter that built the model, for the same reason I stated the symbolism rule above.
:::

::: whereweare
**Where we are.** *The consummation of the age* is not, in its own words, the destruction of the earth, and Matthew proves the distinction himself by using *kosmos* for the field and *aiōn* for the harvest in adjacent clauses. The phrase occurs four times in Matthew, surrounded each time by related imagery, which shifts the burden onto anyone claiming the occurrences are unrelated. A coherent first-century sequence is now visible in Matthew's own ordering. What is not established is that the sequence is the right reading: chapter 13's judgment content, Daniel 12's resurrection language, and the long reception of Matthew 28 all stand against it and are kept at full strength. Daniel 12:2 says *many*, not *all* — retrieved, recorded, and not yet used.
:::

::: earned
**What it earned.** The question stopped being whether everything ends and became which age reaches its appointed completion. Matthew also named two ages in passing, which is the cleaner question and the one I should have asked first.
:::
