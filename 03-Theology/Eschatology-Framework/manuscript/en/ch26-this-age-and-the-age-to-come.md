# Chapter 26: "this age" and "the age to come"

The last chapter ended on a verse I had walked past while looking at something else. Before Matthew ever writes *the consummation of the age*, Matthew has already said there are two ages.

That is the cleaner question, and I should have asked it first. If *this age* simply means all of ordinary human history up to a final judgment, then the transition I was starting to see in Matthew has a serious problem. If the apostolic writers instead understood themselves to be standing at an overlap between two ages, then *the consummation of the age* may mean something a good deal more specific.

I did not want to decide that in advance. I wanted to follow the language.

## Two ages, and Yeshua is the one counting

> whoever speaks against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven, either in this age [<span class="gr">ἐν τούτῳ τῷ αἰῶνι</span>] or in the age to come [<span class="gr">ἐν τῷ μέλλοντι</span>]. (Matthew 12:32)

The second is not merely *the future*. It is *the coming age* — a named thing, set against a present one.

So Matthew possesses an age-to-age framework before he ever uses the phrase I spent the last chapter on. That is on the surface of the text.

## And the framework is not peculiar to Matthew

> …will not fail to receive a hundredfold now in this time… and in the age to come eternal life. (Mark 10:29–30)

Luke has the same contrast. And then Luke gives the one that presses hardest against where I was heading.

> The sons of this age marry and are given in marriage, but those who are considered worthy to attain to that age and to the resurrection from the dead neither marry nor are given in marriage, for they cannot die anymore, because they are equal to angels and are sons of God, being sons of the resurrection. (Luke 20:34–36)

::: evidence
**How strongly evidence bears — against a purely administrative reading of the transition**

The coming age is here associated with **resurrection from the dead**, and with a condition in which those who attain it **cannot die anymore**.

That prevents reducing the age transition to *the Temple administration changed*. There is an eschatological dimension in the coming age, and Yeshua states it. It is not something a system inferred and then read back in.

**CONTRADICTS** — any version of my developing model that would let the age change be a covenantal-administrative event with no resurrection attached.

Kept at full strength. It becomes this chapter's major unresolved problem, and I would rather end with it unresolved than end with it managed.
:::

## The contrast is temporal, not cosmological

Worth saying plainly, because it is easy to slide past: Yeshua does not say *this earth* against *another earth*. He says *this age* against *that age*. Age to age.

Whatever consequences accompany such a transition are separate questions that have to be established separately. The text gives ages. That is the same discipline Matthew forced on me in the last chapter, when he used one word for the field and another for the harvest — now holding across different authors.

::: commentary
**A detour I took between these two chapters, and what it cost me to learn**

Somewhere around here I got ahead of myself. I had a question — *who is actually alive during the thousand years?* — and it felt like a real problem. If the thousand years began with a resurrection, where do the nations at the end of it come from? Something would have to repopulate the earth.

And I noticed what I had started doing to answer it. I was reaching outward, toward historical anomalies and proposed explanations of the recent past, looking for a mechanism that would solve the difficulty.

Then I went back and read the passage.

Satan is bound *so that he might not deceive the nations any longer, until the thousand years were ended.* Binding him so that he cannot deceive the nations until then rather plainly implies that the nations are there the whole time. The text never says they were resurrected at that point, never says they were created at that point, and never says the earth had been emptied and needed refilling.

**The problem dissolved on retrieval. It had never existed.** And I had been reaching for external explanations to solve a biblical difficulty before anyone had verified that the difficulty was real.

I am keeping this in the book because it is the most instructive mistake I made in this stretch, and because it establishes a firewall I am going to need later: claims about recent history and claims about what a text says are two different classes of evidence, and the first may not be used to settle the second. None of this decides anything about Revelation, which I have not opened and am not going to open early.
:::

## Paul: already enthroned, and still "this age"

> God raised him from the dead and seated him at his right hand in the heavenly places, far above all rule and authority and power and dominion, and above every name that is named, not only in this age but also in the one to come. (Ephesians 1:20–21)

Messiah is already enthroned, and Paul is still calling his own period *this age*.

That complicates tidy timelines in both directions, and I want both columns on the page:

| Cannot be said | Because |
|-------------------|-------------------------------|
| Messiah becomes King only when the coming age begins | Paul already portrays Him as enthroned |
| Messiah's resurrection instantly ended the previous age | Paul still distinguishes this age from the one to come, after the resurrection |

Something has genuinely begun without everything belonging to the previous order having reached its conclusion. By now that pattern is not a surprise: it is what Part III found about fulfilment and abolition, and what Part IV found about mission and completion. It keeps being transition rather than instant replacement.

## Paul locates his own generation, and the verb decides it

> …written down for our instruction, on whom the ends of the ages have come. (1 Corinthians 10:11)

In English that reads like a flourish. The Greek is harder to soften.

::: textualnote
**Textual note — <span class="gr">τὰ τέλη τῶν αἰώνων</span> and the tense of <span class="gr">κατήντηκεν</span>**

| Element | Form | Force |
|-----------------|-------------------|--------------------|
| <span class="gr">τέλη</span> *(telē)* | plural of *telos* | ends, culminations |
| <span class="gr">αἰώνων</span> *(aiōnōn)* | plural of *aiōn* | of the ages |
| <span class="gr">κατήντηκεν</span> *(katēntēken)* | **perfect indicative active** | have arrived, and the arrival stands |

The perfect is completed action with continuing present relevance. And the pronoun is *us*.

Paul does not write that the ends of the ages will eventually come upon people many centuries later. He places his own community at the arrival point.

**What this establishes, stated exactly:** apostolic self-location. Paul considered his own generation eschatologically transitional. That is a textual observation before it is anyone's interpretation, and it is **not** proof of any particular date.
:::

One more thing about that verse, recorded rather than used. *Telē* is the plural of <span class="gr">τέλος</span> — the same lexeme as Matthew 24:14's *the end* and Romans 10:4's *end of the law*. This is its third load-bearing appearance in my investigation. Chapter 17 is where I learned what happens when a shared lemma gets quietly counted as corroboration, and I am not doing that again. Same word, three contexts, three meanings to be established separately.

## Hebrews arrives at the same place from a different direction

> he has appeared once for all at the end of the ages to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself. (Hebrews 9:26)

Paul says the ends of the ages have come upon *us*. Hebrews places Messiah's appearance *at the end of the ages*. Two authors, independently, putting the decisive events at a culmination rather than at the opening of a long ordinary stretch.

## And Hebrews 6 produces the overlap out of the text itself

> those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift, and have shared in the Holy Spirit, and have tasted the goodness of the word of God and the powers of the age to come. (Hebrews 6:4–5)

The coming age has not fully arrived. First-century believers have already tasted its powers.

That is an overlap emerging from a sentence rather than an overlap invented to reconcile passages that would not otherwise fit. The difference between those two things is the difference between reading a text and managing one.

## A distinction I am adopting, and a system I am not

The standard term for this is *inaugurated eschatology*: a promised reality has genuinely begun while aspects of its consummation remain outstanding. Often shortened to *already, but not yet*.

I am adopting the distinction and attaching no system to it, because the concept is not the property of either side of this argument. A futurist and a preterist can both agree that Messiah inaugurated realities before their consummation, and then disagree entirely about when the consummation occurred or will occur. Which is precisely the question I am still inside.

## The finding this chapter did not want

> For it was not to angels that God subjected the world to come, of which we are speaking. (Hebrews 2:5)

I checked the Greek because the phrase looked ordinary. It is not ordinary.

::: evidence
**How strongly evidence bears — a correction to my own earlier scope work**

The word is <span class="gr">οἰκουμένην τὴν μέλλουσαν</span> *(oikoumenēn tēn mellousan)* — **the inhabited world to come.**

<span class="gr">οἰκουμένη</span> is Matthew 24:14's word. The word I spent Part II and Part IV establishing need not mean every location on the earth. And Hebrews attaches it to a **coming** order.

**CONTRADICTS** — any use of my scope work that would let *oikoumenē* simply mean *the first-century Roman inhabited world* wherever it appears.

Neither observation cancels the other. Luke's census and famine still show the word can carry a limited referent; Hebrews shows the same word can denote a future order. Together they mean the referent has to be determined at each occurrence rather than fixed once and reused.

That is the scope test doing exactly what I built it to do, and this time it is doing it to me.
:::

## Hebrews 8:13, and the brake I am putting on it

> what is becoming obsolete and growing old is ready to vanish away. (Hebrews 8:13)

*Becoming* obsolete. *Growing* old. *Ready* to vanish. Something new already exists while something old has not finished disappearing — which fits the age-overlap structure so well that I want to use it.

I am not going to. Hebrews 8 is explicitly discussing **covenant**. Treating covenant and age as the same structure would be the cleanest move available to me here, and I have not proved they are the same structure. Two things with the same shape are not thereby the same thing. The correspondence is suggestive and it stays suggestive.

## What is converging, laid out rather than resolved

| Text | What it connects |
|------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| Matthew 13 | consummation, harvest, Daniel-like shining |
| Luke 20 | the coming age, resurrection from the dead |
| Daniel 12 | the time of the end, awakening from the dust, the righteous shining |

These are converging on the same place, and it is not a place I chose. It is resurrection.

And there may be a harder test than Daniel waiting:

> Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep… But each in his own order: Christ the firstfruits, then at his coming those who belong to Christ. Then comes the end, when he delivers the kingdom to God the Father after destroying every rule and every authority and power. For he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet. The last enemy to be destroyed is death. (1 Corinthians 15:20–26)

Resurrection, coming, kingdom and *the end*, in a stated order, with a stated final item. Whatever chronology I eventually build has to survive that paragraph. It may be a harder test than Daniel 12, and the hardest texts are the ones worth having.

## What this chapter does and does not establish

**Established.** The New Testament explicitly distinguishes this age from the coming age, across Matthew, Mark, Luke, Paul and Hebrews. Apostolic writers locate themselves at the ends of the ages, with 1 Corinthians 10:11 in the perfect tense. Messiah is already raised and enthroned during that period. First-century believers already experience powers belonging to the coming age.

**Strongly suggested.** The New Testament contains an overlapping, inaugurated age structure.

**Plausible and not established.** That the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple marks the decisive consummation of *this age*. I have a model and I do not have a demonstration.

**Unresolved and pressing.** The coming age is associated with resurrection, and I have reconciled that with nothing. Hebrews 2:5 has corrected how I may use my own scope work.

## The chain that got me here

I started by asking whether I was personally commanded to proclaim the good news everywhere because Matthew 24:14 had not happened yet.

To answer it I had to ask what *world* meant. That forced audience and geography. Those forced Israel and the nations. That forced covenant. Covenant forced Torah and the new covenant. That forced Matthew 24. Matthew 24 forced the Danielic judgment language. That forced *the end of the age*.

And *the end of the age* has now forced resurrection.

That is not wandering. Each question earned the next one, and none of them was chosen because it looked helpful.

::: whereweare
**Where we are.** The two-age framework is explicit across five New Testament witnesses, and the apostolic writers place themselves at the culmination of the ages rather than at the start of a long ordinary stretch — 1 Corinthians 10:11's perfect tense makes that hard to soften. The overlap is not imported: Hebrews 6 states that believers have already tasted the powers of the coming age while it remains coming. What is not established is when the present age reaches its consummation. Against a first-century answer stand Luke 20's association of the coming age with resurrection, 1 Corinthians 15's stated order ending with death itself, and Hebrews 2:5, which uses Matthew 24:14's own word for a world still to come and corrects my earlier scope work in the process.
:::

::: earned
**What it earned.** Resurrection stopped being a topic I would reach eventually and became the next thing I am required to take. And the order matters: if I settle the age question first and then interpret resurrection to fit, I will have done the one thing this whole method exists to prevent.
:::
