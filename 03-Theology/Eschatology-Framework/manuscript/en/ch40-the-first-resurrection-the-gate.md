# Chapter 40: the first resurrection — the gate

Everything in this Part is an attack on my own reconstruction.

I want that stated plainly at the top, because a reader who has come this far has watched a case accumulate, and the natural expectation now is a chapter that starts converting accumulation into conclusion. That is not what happens next. What happens next is twelve stages of walking at my own model with the best weapons I could find, and this Part reports them in the order they landed.

Some of them landed. A verb I had asserted for months turned out not to exist in the passage I had asserted it about. A bridge I needed weakened exactly where I needed it. Four separate doors I tried to open, I closed myself.

I do not yet know, writing this, whether the hypothesis survives. That is not a literary pose — it is the actual epistemic position of the investigation at this point, and the only reason this Part is worth reading is that I have not gone back and tidied it afterwards.

And it begins at a gate that the previous Part built and left standing.

> **Does Scripture permit the first resurrection of Revelation 20 to have already occurred — and what exactly would that event have involved?**

The historical work cannot move until that is answered. So it gets answered first, badly for me if necessary.

## I argued from incomplete text, and said so before arguing

::: textualnote
**Declared before any argument rested on it — and what became of it**

Three passages central to this chapter were, when this work was done, **only partly in hand.**

| Passage | What I had | What was missing |
|-------------------------|--------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **Revelation 6:9–11** | verse 9 | ❗ **verses 10–11 — the *how long* cry, the white robes, and *rest a little longer*** |
| **Matthew 27:51–53** | through *the tombs also were opened* | ❗ ***many bodies of the saints who had fallen asleep were raised*** |
| **Hebrews 12:22–23** | through *the assembly of the firstborn* | ❗ ***and to the spirits of the righteous made perfect*** |

All three are load-bearing here. The first is worse than that: **Revelation 6:10–11 is the passage this chapter's central argument leans on.**

So they were treated as **reported, not verified**, and the argument was built at that reduced strength rather than at the strength I wanted it to have.

### ✅ **All three have since been verified in full — and the Revelation 6 result is the one worth stopping on.**

❗❗ **The verses that arrived late did not merely permit the control built without them. They strengthened it.** They give the martyrs a *how long*, a white robe, and an instruction to **rest yet a little time** — <span class="gr">ἔτι χρόνον μικρόν</span> — **until** their number should be **fulfilled**, <span class="gr">ἕως πληρωθῶσιν</span>. **Every one of those is conscious, addressed, waiting existence**, which is precisely what the control needed and could not previously assert.

📌 **And that outcome was only available because the gap was declared instead of filled in from expectation.** Had I written the confident version and let the verses arrive afterwards, I would never have known whether I had read them or remembered them.

I am declaring it at the top rather than letting a reader discover it at the bottom — the same discipline as the Revelation 17 numbering drift and the interlinear transliteration fault. A chapter that quietly borrows the authority of verification it does not have is worse than a chapter with a hole in it, because only one of the two can be repaired.
:::

## Two corrections, and they run in opposite directions

Before the synthesis, two things I had to fix. One came from me being wrong; the other came from me being wrong in the other direction, which is the harder one to notice.

::: commentary
**Correction one — stop reopening what Revelation already settled**

I kept re-asking whether Revelation 20's two resurrections are really distinct.

They are, and **Revelation stages them itself.** A blessed and holy group live and reign with Messiah for the thousand years, over whom the second death has no jurisdiction. *The rest of the dead* do not live until the thousand years are finished, and the great white throne follows the little season.

That is textually established. Continuing to phrase the question as though the distinction were under review wastes the reader's time and mine, and it creates the false impression that the two-stage structure is something I am arguing *for* rather than something the text handed me.

**The real synthesis question is narrower:** how does the broader resurrection language of Daniel, Yeshua and Paul map onto a two-stage structure Revelation has already established?

**Correction two — and this one is against my own enthusiasm**

I had described the first-resurrection saints as *morally higher* than those judged later.

That goes beyond what Revelation says. It establishes distinctive **holiness, status and reward** — blessed and holy, priests of God and of Messiah, reigning, immune from the second death. All of that is there.

What is not there is a moral ranking of everyone else. The later great-white-throne population includes **everyone** in that horizon — and **the book of life is present at that judgment.** So I may not infer that every person appearing there is morally inferior in some simple ranked sense.

Both corrections go in. A record that only preserved the corrections that made me look careful would be a different kind of document.
:::

## What any compared text has to respect

Revelation 20 supplies the controlling shape, and it has three features. Anything I compare to it has to answer to all three:

**a specified faithful population · life or resurrection before the thousand years · remaining dead afterwards.**

Now the comparisons, in order, with the ones that hurt kept in.

## Daniel 12, and a word I already lost

Daniel 12:2 gives *many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake*, some to everlasting life and some to shame and everlasting contempt. Dust and awaking evoke the dead returning, not moral drowsiness.

And *many* — <span class="he">רַבִּים</span> — is the word I was tempted by four Parts ago and that Part V finally adjudicated.

**It went against me.** Biblical *many* can function as a broad collective rather than meaning *some but definitely not all*, so it does **not** establish a limited resurrection population. I recorded that adjudication when it cost me something, and I am not reopening it here because Revelation 20 has since made a limited population look attractive. A word does not become more helpful because I need it more.

What Daniel 12 does give is the conceptual ancestry. Unprecedented trouble, Michael, deliverance of those written in the book, many awakening, the wise shining, time and times and half a time — Revelation reuses every one of those categories. So Daniel 12 is almost certainly in Revelation 20's background, and the live question is whether Revelation is **expanding** Daniel's single resurrection into stages. Plausible. Revelation demonstrably expands prophetic images rather than merely copying them.

## Matthew 27 proves a category, and does not supply an identification

::: textualnote
**The sentence this section turns on — once reported, now verified.**

Matthew 27:52 reads that **many bodies** — <span class="gr">πολλὰ σώματα</span> — of the saints who had fallen asleep **were raised**, <span class="gr">ἠγέρθησαν</span>; and 27:53 places their entering the holy city and appearing to many **after His resurrection**, <span class="gr">μετὰ τὴν ἔγερσιν αὐτοῦ</span>.

⚠️ **I record that this section was written while the clause was still only reported.** The reader is entitled to know which sentences were load-bearing before the text arrived to support them.
:::

So Scripture already records a **partial bodily resurrection of saints inside ordinary history**. Bodies, not visions.

Which proves a category I need: **bodily resurrection does not automatically have to mean the final resurrection of all humanity.** Something can be genuinely bodily, genuinely historical, and genuinely bounded.

And it is probably **not** Revelation 20's first resurrection. Revelation 20's population is defined by those killed for Yeshua's testimony and by refusing beast worship and the mark — conditions belonging after Yeshua's death, inside the persecution Revelation tracks. Matthew 27's saints died before any of that.

So it establishes the possibility of a historically bounded resurrection event. It does not establish this one. Those are different findings and I have spent six Parts learning not to merge them.

## John 5 looks universal, and John 6 is the real obstacle

> An hour is coming when **all who are in the tombs** will hear his voice and come out, those who have done good to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment. (John 5:28–29)

That sounds like the great-white-throne horizon, not a limited martyr resurrection.

Revelation supplies a way to relate them: John 5 may **compress** the whole resurrection-and-judgment reality into one *hour* while Revelation supplies internal stages. Prophetic and apocalyptic discourse demonstrably compresses extended periods — I established that about Revelation 12 itself, where birth runs straight to enthronement.

But that is a **hypothesis**, and I have to state its limit in the same breath: John 5 does not require both populations to rise at the same instant, and I may not assert the opposite either.

And then John 6, which is the harder problem.

::: openquestion
**Open question — *the last day***

John 6 says repeatedly that Messiah will raise his own **on the last day**.

If a first resurrection precedes a thousand-year interval and the general resurrection follows it, what does *the last day* mean?

Candidates: the final day of an age · the final resurrection horizon considered corporately · the actual final consummative day · language Revelation later expands internally.

🛑 **This is a genuine obstacle to careless staging, and it is an obstacle to mine.** It needs source-language and Johannine-context work. I am not going to treat *last day* as self-defining, in either direction — and John 6 has not been retrieved, so it stands here as a flagged problem rather than a solved one.
:::

## Paul already stages it

> Christ has been raised from the dead, **the firstfruits** of those who have fallen asleep… But **each in his own order**: Christ the firstfruits, then at his coming those who belong to Christ. Then comes the end… (1 Corinthians 15:20, 23–24)

::: finding
**Finding — resurrection staging is not foreign to Paul**

Messiah → those who belong to him at his coming → *to telos*, the end. That is a staged sequence, in Paul's own words, with his own ordering term.

So *do not flatten these into one moment without argument* is not special pleading invented for Revelation 20. Paul flattened nothing.

And the last enemy destroyed is death — which sits **inside** Paul's ordered sequence and belongs to its consummation, and which Part VI already aligned with Revelation 20:14. *(Paul's syntax at 15:24–26 will not yield a finer chronology than that, and I am not going to squeeze one out of it: the reign, the subjection of the enemies and death's abolition are given as the terminal movement, not as dated steps after it.)*

⚠️ **But the pressure runs back the other way in the same passage.** *Those who belong to Christ* sounds broad. It does not sound like *some martyrs now, other believers a thousand years later.*

Three options, and I am not choosing: the martyrs **represent** Messiah's faithful corporately · Paul **compresses** several stages · or **I am misreading Revelation 20's population as limited** when John is highlighting martyrs within a larger group.

📌 That last one is live, and it is not a matter of preference. Revelation 20:4's syntax is genuinely disputed as to whether it describes one class further described or more than one. **That requires grammatical authority, not my own parsing** — and if the first resurrection includes all who refused beast allegiance rather than only the beheaded, the population is much broader than I have been assuming.
:::

## And then Paul raises the bill

> We shall not all sleep, but **we shall all be changed**, in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet. For the trumpet will sound, and the dead will be raised imperishable, **and we shall be changed**. (1 Corinthians 15:51–52)

> The dead in Christ will rise **first**. Then we who are alive, who are left, will be **caught up** together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air. (1 Thessalonians 4:16–17)

Paul distinguishes **dead raised** from **living changed**. The event is not merely resurrection of corpses.

One brake before the burden. Paul's *first* in 1 Thessalonians is relative **to the living** — the dead rise before the living are caught up, within that sequence. It is not automatically *the first resurrection in all eschatological chronology*, and I may not treat <span class="gr">πρῶτον</span> as lexically identical to Revelation's <span class="gr">ἀνάστασις ἡ πρώτη</span>. Correspondence, not identity. The rule applies here exactly as it applied when it was helping me.

::: evidence
**How strongly evidence bears — and this is a falsification criterion, not a difficulty**

**If** Revelation 20's first resurrection is identified with 1 Corinthians 15 and 1 Thessalonians 4, then a past-millennium model may require **dead saints raised *and* living saints instantaneously transformed.**

That is far harder to hide historically than a purely heavenly vindication vision would be.

The available escapes, stated so they can be examined rather than assumed: Paul and Revelation describe **related but not identical** events · the transformation **removes participants from ordinary visibility** · the event is **geographically or covenantally localized** · or I have **misunderstood the temporal relationship** between them.

🛑 **None of those can be settled by speculation, and I am not permitted to pick whichever one rescues the model.** This is now a falsification criterion for the hypothesis, and it is the largest one this investigation has written down.
:::

## The strongest thing in this chapter, and it is against me

Here is the argument I did not want to find.

A past millennium would be enormously simpler if *first resurrection* just meant that the martyrs went to heaven when they died. That reading costs nothing historically. It asks for no missing event. It is the escape hatch, and I would very much like to walk through it.

Revelation closes it.

> I saw under the altar **the souls of those who had been slain** for the word of God and for the witness they had borne. (Revelation 6:9)

::: finding
**Finding — the martyrs were already alive, and Revelation says so fourteen chapters early**

The martyrs did not need chapter 20 in order to exist consciously after death. John had already seen them in chapter 6 — souls beneath the altar, **speaking**, **receiving white robes**, **being told to wait**. Revelation 7 similarly shows the faithful before the throne.

✅ The cry, the robes and *rest a little longer* are in verses 10–11 — **the part I did not have when this control was first written, and which has since been verified in full.** The argument that was labelled *strong and not yet confirmed* now stands at full strength, and it got there without a word of it being adjusted to fit.

**The control:** Revelation 6 already depicts slain martyrs consciously existing before Revelation 20. Therefore Revelation 20's *they lived* and *first resurrection* **may not be reduced to conscious postmortem survival without explaining the change of state.**

They already have conscious postmortem survival. Chapter 20 gives them something else.

**And this is Revelation-internal.** I did not import a doctrine of the intermediate state from a systematic theology and use it to pressure John. John supplied both halves, in his own book, and the earlier half arrives thirteen chapters before the verse it constrains.
:::

There is a second, independent argument in the same direction, and it is also now verified. **Hebrews 12:23 describes *the spirits of the righteous made perfect* in heavenly assembly imagery — and does not call it resurrection.** So conscious heavenly existence is not automatically resurrection, said by a different author in a different book. ⚠️ **The English is verified; I have not read the Greek of the verse, so no lexical weight is placed on it here.**

## A translation artifact, caught by machinery built one stage earlier

::: wordstudy
**Word study — <span class="gr">ἀνάστασις</span> at Hebrews 11:35** `ENGLISH-SPECIFIC`

> Some were tortured, refusing to accept release, so that they might **rise again to a better life**. (Hebrews 11:35, ESV)

The Greek is <span class="gr">κρείττονος ἀναστάσεως</span> — *kreittonos anastaseōs*, **a better resurrection**. Other translations render it exactly that way. ⚠️ **The English is verified; the Greek itself I have not read**, and the point waits on it.

**The ESV renders <span class="gr">ἀνάστασις</span> as *life*, not *resurrection*.**

And this chapter's entire argument turns on **differentiated resurrection quality or status** — which is precisely what that rendering obscures.

📌 This is a textbook translation artifact, and it was caught by the scope-tagging machinery written one stage before it. The rules were built for a Spanish problem; the first thing they caught was an English one.

If it holds: early believers could speak of resurrection with differentiated quality or status, which is conceptual support for Revelation's staging. ⚠️ **Hebrews supplies no millennium chronology.** Conceptual support, not a timetable.
:::

## Four models, and none of them selected

::: claimtypes
**The four readings that survive this chapter**

**A — bodily first resurrection before the millennium.** Revelation means what it strongly appears to mean.
*Fits:* <span class="gr">ἔζησαν</span> · <span class="gr">ἀνάστασις</span> · *the rest of the dead* later · the change-of-state argument from chapter 6.
*Cost for a past millennium:* ❗ **when and where did it happen?**

**B — heavenly vindication.** *First resurrection* is symbolic for martyrs entering heavenly reign at death.
*Advantage:* historically far easier — which is exactly why I am suspicious of my own attraction to it.
*Problems:* Revelation 6 already has them conscious · the same verb is used of the rest of the dead · explicit resurrection terminology · Paul's bodily framework.
**Currently under considerable textual pressure.**

**C — bodily resurrection plus living transformation.** Revelation 20 covers the dead; Paul supplies the simultaneously changed living.
*Harmonizes well.* ❗ *And makes the historical burden larger, not smaller.*

**D — the martyrs represent a larger corporate faithful population.** Revelation highlights martyr witnesses but represents the broader people belonging to Messiah.
*Possible.* Waits on the disputed syntax of 20:4 and on grammatical authority I do not have.

**None selected.** What the current textual weight favours is narrower than any of the four: **some form of actual resurrection or change of state substantially stronger than mere postmortem heavenly survival.**
:::

## "Spiritual body" does not mean no body

> What is sown is perishable; what is raised is imperishable… It is sown **a natural body**; it is raised **a spiritual body**. (1 Corinthians 15:42, 44)

The Greek is <span class="gr">σῶμα ψυχικόν</span> against <span class="gr">σῶμα πνευματικόν</span>. ⚠️ **The English is verified; the Greek is reported.**

**Both contain <span class="gr">σῶμα</span>.** Body.

::: commentary
**Rule — transformed embodiment**

A spiritual body is not an absent body. It is a body animated and ordered by the Spirit, against the present mortal condition.

So the real alternative is **not** *a physical corpse walking around normally* versus *a purely spiritual metaphor*. Paul supplies a third category: **resurrected, transformed embodiment.**

And Yeshua after His resurrection is the pattern — appearing, disappearing from immediate perception, entering closed rooms, eating, being touched, and finally ascending. ⚠️ **Reported, not verified.** **Paul explicitly makes Him the firstfruits, which means He is the controlling pattern here and not an escape hatch.**

The distinction matters: a controlling pattern constrains what I may say. An escape hatch would let me say anything.
:::

## And the brake that keeps this falsifiable

Transformed embodiment means I may not assume raised saints would interact with ordinary mortals in ordinary ways. So their absence from ordinary records is not automatically fatal to a past first resurrection.

That is a real answer to a real objection. It is also the exact point where a hypothesis quietly stops being falsifiable, and I can feel the pull.

::: finding
**New rule — the Supernatural-Possibility Brake**

**A biblical precedent showing that an event *could* occur supernaturally may answer an objection of impossibility. It does not constitute evidence that the proposed historical event actually occurred.**

Supernatural possibility may not be converted into an evidence-erasing device.

**And the corollary, for the historical work this Part is heading toward:** absence of evidence has weight only relative to the amount and type of evidence we should reasonably expect to survive. That has to be **argued**, carefully, and not asserted — in either direction.

This rule is aimed at me. It exists because I noticed, while writing the previous section, that I had just been handed something that could excuse any missing evidence forever, and that I was pleased about it.
:::

## The order the remaining work has to run in

One last thing, and it is procedural rather than exegetical, which is why it is easy to get wrong.

🛑 **The historical investigation may not begin with *find a thousand years somewhere*.**

**First:** determine the first-resurrection and gathering event.

**Second:** determine Satan's binding marker — constrained by what Revelation actually said the binding does, which is that it should be identifiable as a **change in the deception of the nations**.

**Third:** only then ask whether those two align historically.

**Fourth:** if they do, measure the interval.

Reverse that order and the procedure becomes: find an attractive millennium in history, then retrofit a resurrection and a binding around it. That is not research. That is decoration with citations, and it would be indistinguishable from the thing I have spent nine Parts trying not to do.

::: whereweare
**Where we are.** The gate is open but not cleared. Revelation's two-stage structure is settled and is not being reopened; the question is how Daniel, Yeshua and Paul map onto it. Daniel supplies ancestry, and the one word that would have helped was already adjudicated against me in Part V. Matthew 27 proves that a bodily resurrection can be historically bounded, without identifying this one. Paul stages resurrection himself — and then raises the burden by adding transformed living saints to it, which is far harder to hide than a heavenly vision. The strongest finding in the chapter is Revelation-internal and closes the escape I would most like: the martyrs were already conscious in chapter 6, so *they lived* in chapter 20 has to mark a change of state. Four models remain live and none is selected. What the textual weight currently favours is an actual change of state substantially stronger than postmortem survival — which means the historical burden is real, and it is mine.
:::
