# The biblical story in one map

The Bible begins in Genesis, with creation. This map begins a little later, in Genesis 12, because that is where the thread this book follows begins: Yah's promise to Abraham that all the families of the earth would be blessed through him. That promise runs through Israel, the covenant, the prophets and David to Yeshua HaMashiach, and from Him out to the nations.

This book asks two questions about that thread. Who is meant to carry the message of Yeshua HaMashiach today? And how much of what the prophets said about the promise has already been fulfilled, and how much is still ahead? The book moves through the whole Bible to answer them, and it does not always move in order. If you lose your place, come back to this page.

Here is the thread in one line. Everything else in this book hangs somewhere on it:

> **Abraham → Isaac → Jacob/Israel → covenant people → Torah and Prophets → Davidic promises → Messianic expectation → Yeshua HaMashiach → apostolic mission → Gentiles grafted in.**

And here is the same story with its landmarks. Dates are approximate. Several of the early ones are debated, and nothing in this book depends on settling them.

| When | What happens | Where it matters most in this book |
|---|---|---|
| Early second millennium BC *(traditional; debated)* | Yah calls **Abraham** and promises that all the families of the earth will be blessed through him (Genesis 12:3). The promise passes to **Isaac**, then to **Jacob**, renamed **Israel**. | Parts I, III and IV |
| *(date debated)* | **Moses** leads Israel out of Egypt. At Sinai Yah gives the **Torah** and makes a covenant with the people. | Part III |
| About 1000 BC | **David** is king. Yah promises him a son whose kingdom will last (2 Samuel 7). | background |
| 722 BC | Assyria destroys the northern kingdom of Israel. | background |
| 605–586 BC | Babylon takes Jerusalem, carries its people into **exile**, and destroys the first temple. **Daniel** and **Ezekiel** write from among the exiles. | Parts III, VI and VIII |
| From 538 BC | Exiles return. The **second temple** is finished around 516 BC. | background |
| 3rd–2nd century BC | The Hebrew Scriptures are translated into Greek in Egypt: the **Septuagint**. The New Testament writers often quote it. | Parts IV, VII and VIII |
| From 63 BC | **Rome** rules the land of Israel. Many in Israel are waiting for the **Messiah**. | Parts II, IV and VI |
| Around AD 30 | **Yeshua** teaches, is crucified, and rises. On the Mount of Olives He tells His disciples that the temple will fall (Matthew 24). | Parts II, V and VII |
| AD 30s–60s | The **apostles** carry the message from Jerusalem outward. **Gentiles** — non-Jews — are brought in, which Paul pictures as wild branches **grafted into Israel's olive tree** (Romans 11). | Parts I, IV and IX |
| AD 70 | Rome destroys **Jerusalem and the temple**. | Parts II and V |
| AD 95–96 *(traditional)*, or before AD 70 *(minority view)* | **John** writes **Revelation**. When it was written is disputed, and the book weighs the evidence instead of choosing the date it would prefer. | Parts VI and VII |

Three things on this map matter more than the rest:

1. **The promise reached the nations from the start.** Abraham's promise already includes all the families of the earth. Gentile inclusion is not an afterthought.
2. **The Septuagint.** Much of the argument turns on exact wording, and the New Testament often quotes the Greek translation rather than the Hebrew. When the two differ, the book says so.
3. **AD 70.** A great deal of prophecy was spoken to people who lived to see Jerusalem fall. The book asks, passage by passage, how much of it was aimed there.
