# What is the Bible?

This book reads the Bible closely, so it helps to know what is actually being read.

## Two collections

**The Hebrew Scriptures.** Jewish tradition calls them the **Tanakh**, a word built from the first letters of its three parts:

- **Torah** — *instruction*. The five books of Moses, Genesis to Deuteronomy.
- **Nevi'im** — *the Prophets*. Joshua and Judges, Samuel and Kings, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the twelve shorter prophets.
- **Ketuvim** — *the Writings*. Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Daniel, Chronicles and others.

These are written in Hebrew, with a few sections (parts of Daniel and Ezra) in Aramaic. Protestant Bibles call the same books the **Old Testament** and arrange them in a different order. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include some additional books.

**The New Testament.** Twenty-seven books, written in Greek in the first century: four accounts of Yeshua's life (the Gospels), Acts, letters from Paul and other apostles, and Revelation.

## A third text you will meet: the Septuagint

Before Yeshua was born, the Hebrew Scriptures were translated into Greek for Greek-speaking Jews. That translation is called the **Septuagint**. The New Testament writers quote it often, and sometimes its wording differs from the Hebrew. For the book of Daniel there are even two Greek versions, an older one and one associated with a translator named **Theodotion**. Several chapters turn on which Greek Daniel a New Testament writer was quoting.

## No originals, many copies

None of the original documents survive. What survives is copies, and copies of copies. For the New Testament they number in the thousands. Where the copies disagree, the difference is called a **textual variant**. Most variants are spelling or word order and change nothing. A few matter. When one bears on the argument, this book stops and shows you. One example is Revelation 5:10, where the manuscripts disagree on whether the saints *reign* or *will reign*.

Scholars compare the copies and publish a **critical edition**: their best reconstruction of the original wording. For the Greek New Testament this book uses the **SBL Greek New Testament**.

## Every translation is a set of decisions

An English or Spanish Bible is a translation, and translators have to choose. Two good translations can map the same Greek words in opposite directions. Romans 10:18 is an example this book examines, and only the Greek shows which English word belongs to which Greek one. That is why, where the wording carries the argument, the book prints the original and tells you what it establishes, what it merely allows, and what it does not establish.

## What the Bible is, in this book

The investigation treats Scripture as the foundation for deciding whether something is true. It is not necessarily where a question starts. A question can begin in a conversation, a news story or a book. It gets settled by what the text actually says, read in its own language and its own setting.
